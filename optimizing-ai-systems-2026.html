Optimizing AI systems from silicon to useful work

A system can generate more tokens and still create less value. The correct optimisation target combines task quality, latency, throughput, energy, cost, and reliability—then traces the bottleneck through model architecture, software, memory, interconnect, racks, and power.

The unit of analysis is the whole system.

Local gains multiply only when they remain compatible. A faster kernel may move the bottleneck into memory; a cheaper model may reduce task success; a high-throughput configuration may be unusably slow for an interactive agent.

The thesis in four statements

AI efficiency is not a single hardware number. It is a chain of coupled design choices whose value depends on the workload and the economic cost of waiting, failing, or consuming scarce capacity.

1. Optimise outcomes

Tokens, FLOPs and accelerator utilisation are intermediate measures. The economically relevant numerator is a quality-adjusted completed task.

2. Choose a service point

Interactive coding, voice, and agents value latency. Offline indexing and document processing value aggregate throughput and low unit cost.

3. Co-design the stack

Model shape, precision, kernels, memory layout, parallelism, networking, cooling, and power must work together. Isolated improvements can cancel one another.

4. Re-benchmark continuously

Frameworks, kernels, drivers, quantisation, schedulers, and models change. A benchmark is a dated configuration—not a permanent property of a chip.

Useful cognitive work ÷ (pounds × joules × seconds × operational risk)

This is a decision framework, not a literal universal scalar. Each deployment must define “useful work,” acceptable quality, latency objectives, and failure costs.

Optimisation levers and their hidden costs

No technique is universally beneficial. Each one targets a particular bottleneck and must be evaluated at an explicit quality and service level.

Model and algorithm

Levers
Distillation, sparse experts, latent attention, quantisation, multi-token prediction, speculative decoding and cascades.
Potential gain
Less active compute, less memory traffic, fewer sequential decode steps or better quality at a fixed budget.
Hidden cost
Calibration error, rejected drafts, quality loss, routing overhead, extra training complexity or hardware-specific affinity.

Runtime and scheduling

Levers
Continuous batching, paged KV cache, prefix caching, chunked prefill, disaggregated serving and request routing.
Potential gain
Higher utilisation, lower fragmentation, better tail latency and different hardware for prefill versus decode.
Hidden cost
Queueing, operational complexity, network traffic, cache invalidation and failure recovery across more components.

Hardware and facility

Levers
Accelerator choice, HBM, scale-up fabric, scale-out network, power density, liquid cooling and geographic placement.
Potential gain
More usable bandwidth, larger models per node, lower communication time and more work per provisioned megawatt.
Hidden cost
Vendor lock-in, stranded capacity, utilisation risk, long lead times, power constraints and financing exposure.

A reproducible benchmark protocol

The purpose of benchmarking is not to crown a permanent winner. It is to find the efficient operating region for a defined workload and to make the result reproducible.

Minimum experiment specification

  1. Freeze model, checkpoint, tokenizer, quality target and decoding policy.
  2. State input/output lengths and their distributions, not only averages.
  3. Report hardware count, topology, precision, framework, kernels and versions.
  4. Sweep concurrency and batching; do not compare one vendor’s optimum with another’s default.
  5. Measure TTFT, TPOT, throughput, p95/p99 latency, power and error rate.
  6. Calculate cost per successful task and per service-level-compliant request.
  7. Publish configuration and date; rerun after material software changes.

Decision questions

  • What user delay is economically acceptable?
  • Is the workload prompt-heavy, decode-heavy, tool-heavy or multimodal?
  • Does quality remain constant after quantisation or model substitution?
  • Does utilisation survive real traffic variability and tail latency?
  • Which bottleneck appears next if the present one is removed?
  • What is the value of optionality versus a hardware-specific optimisation?
  • How much engineering and operational complexity is being capitalised off-book?

Bottom line

The best AI system is not the one with the highest advertised peak. It is the lowest-risk configuration on the quality–latency–throughput–cost frontier for the task that matters.

Optimisation should therefore proceed in this order: define useful work, set the service level, profile the bottleneck, co-design across layers, measure the end-to-end result, and repeat as the software stack changes.
