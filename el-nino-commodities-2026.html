01 · Timing & consensus

Not a 2027 onset

NOAA, BoM, JMA, WMO and IRI agree that El Niño began in 2026 and is strengthening. The uncertainty has moved from whether it arrives to where the strongest weather anomalies appear.

Direct answer:

Operational onset was May–June 2026. The highest-probability peak is October–December 2026. If it weakens in boreal spring, a sensible working duration is about 10–12 months, but no agency gives an exact ending month.

What “consensus” actually means

  • NOAA: more than 90% chance of a very strong event in northern autumn/winter.
  • BoM: firmly established, likely to peak in southern late spring/summer and persist into autumn 2027.
  • JMA: 100% probability through boreal winter; model magnitude comparable with 1997.
  • IRI: 23 of 26 models reach very strong at peak; objective ensemble remains above 90% through Mar–May.

Metrics differ: NOAA uses RONI, JMA Niño 3, while other centres often use Niño 3.4. The numerical levels are not perfectly interchangeable.

02 · Transcript audit

Directionally right, too deterministic

The Bloomberg transcript was a useful thesis prompt. Current primary evidence confirms the exceptional ENSO setup, but not a guaranteed global commodity shock—and especially not a one-factor cocoa replay.

What the transcript implied

A powerful event drives widespread costs

  • Pacific warming above 2°C by year-end.
  • West African cocoa already losing yield.
  • Panama drought pushes LNG and energy prices.
  • Local crop losses translate into global inflation.

What is verified now

The climate signal is strong; the price signal is conditional

  • Official forecasts now support a very strong late-2026 peak.
  • ICCO says the 2023/24 cocoa deficit was not directly attributable to El Niño.
  • Panama and energy are second-order, multi-causal channels.
  • Five of six post-1980 El Niño episodes in a World Bank event study had flat or falling agricultural prices.

03 · Transmission

Six gates between ocean and return

The event label is only the first gate. Every link can amplify, offset or break the trade.

1 · ClimateENSO strengthAlready public and highly certain
2 · RegionRain & heatTeleconnection is probabilistic
3 · CropCritical windowStage and timing determine damage
4 · BalanceOutput & stocksOffsets and policy can absorb loss
5 · MarketFutures curveForecast may be priced before harvest
6 · ProductETC returnRoll, swap, FX and spread intervene

Published crop evidence makes the same point: El Niño improves global mean soybean yield in some estimates, while maize, rice and wheat show mixed global averages because gains and losses occur in different places. See Iizumi et al., Nature Communications.

Why 2024 is the wrong single-factor analogue

ICCO attributes the 2023/24 deficit to adverse weather plus black pod disease, swollen-shoot virus and structural constraints. Its retrospective says the deficit was not directly linked to El Niño as initially expected. For 2024/25, ICCO's May 2026 bulletin estimated a small 48kt surplus and a 28.5% stocks-to-grindings ratio.

Require a multi-signal deterioration before considering COCO:

07 · Evidence gate

Three of five before exposure

Require at least three gates, including an official production downgrade or tight inventory evidence. ENSO strength by itself does not count as a commodity edge.

1 · ForecastSeasonal rain/heat anomaly matches the historical regional pattern.
2 · ObservedRain, soil moisture or heat actually deteriorates during the crop window.
3 · CropUSDA, FAO, ICCO, ICO or another authority cuts output or quality.
4 · BalanceStocks/export availability are tight and the futures curve confirms scarcity.
5 · ProductExact ISIN is eligible, live, liquid and has an acceptable spread.
What is already priced:

The existence and exceptional likely strength of El Niño are public. The research edge—if one emerges—will be in crop timing, inventories and product selection, not in discovering the event.
